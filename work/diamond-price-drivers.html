Diamond price drivers

Clarity moves the price of a pear-shaped diamond more than color does. Across 918 stones listed by four retailers, the two grades together still leave more than a third of the price unexplained.

Spring 2026. Excel, regression, data validation, web scraping.

The question

Two questions, not one. Which published grade moves the price of a pear-shaped diamond more, clarity or color? And do four retailers price the same specification the same way?

The data

918 pear-shaped diamonds listed by four retailers. I scraped the listings, cleaned them into a single workbook, and did no analysis until the cleaning was finished and written down.

Method

A single-variable linear regression of price on clarity, a second on color, then a combined model using both. Confidence intervals on the estimates rather than point values alone.

Separately, pivot tables comparing the four retailers at matched specifications, and a benchmark of retailer pricing against the Rapaport price guide.

Built in Excel. Reported in Word.

What I found

Share of price variation explained, by model
Model R2
Clarity alone 0.40
Color alone 0.29
Clarity and color combined 0.633

Clarity is the stronger single driver, and not by a small margin: it accounts for about half again as much of the variation as color does.

The more useful number is the one left over. Together the two grades explain 63.3% of the variation in price, which means more than a third of what these stones cost is set by something the two headline grades do not capture. Anyone using clarity and color alone to decide whether a price is fair is working with roughly two-thirds of the picture.

On the second question, the retailer benchmark showed a $1,700 per-carat gap against the Rapaport guide.
